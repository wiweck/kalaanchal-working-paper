The starting point

Why clarity has to come before outreach

A first message to a client fixes three things at once: who the client thinks they are dealing with, what they think is on offer, and whose name the relationship is recorded under. Those are hard to change afterwards.

  1. Which organisation are we building?KalaAnchal and Tri-Impact Global have different mandates. The plan reads differently depending on which one is the subject.
  2. Who speaks to clients?The sender of the first message shapes how it is read and where the relationship sits.
  3. What is KalaAnchal, as a start-up?Its definition, its market, its competitors and what sets it apart.
  4. How do we create interest?Tradition-led stories, social media, and an invitation to co-create or commission.
  5. What do we prepare first?The stories, the redesigned website, the way a commission works, and what an investor will look for.

Part 1

Two organisations with different jobs

The distinction

Two entities, two mandates

KalaAnchal is a social impact venture. Living Art Traditions is an initiative of Tri-Impact Global, which is KalaAnchal's social impact partner. The two are planned and judged separately.

KalaAnchal

A social impact venture in art

  • Creates interest in India's living art traditions and brings clients in
  • Co-creates and commissions work with artists and artisans, and delivers it
  • Holds the client relationships and pays the makers
  • Delivers its social impact through Tri-Impact's network
  • Can raise money from impact investors

Tri-Impact Global

A public charitable trust since October 2012, formerly GoodWeave India Trust1

  • Runs Living Art Traditions for artists, artisans and their communities
  • Tells how those artists and communities are being empowered
  • Is the vehicle for grants, CSR, and social and climate initiatives
  • Is KalaAnchal's social impact partner

Part 2

KalaAnchal as a start-up

The starting position

What KalaAnchal starts with

KalaAnchal starts with no stock of artworks. It starts with access to a network of artists and artisans, a partner with a long record, and the traditions those makers hold.

What it has

  • A network of artists and artisans, reached through Tri-Impact's Living Art Traditions
  • Master artists of living traditions among them, with national honours and international shows1
  • A social impact partner with field presence and a 14-year record
  • A curatorial stance: depth over breadth4
  • A base in North India

What it builds next

  • A name that clients recognise
  • Tradition stories that create interest
  • A redesigned website that says what KalaAnchal is and whom it serves
  • Co-creation: combining the skills of different local traditions to make contemporary work
  • A clear way for a client to start a co-creation or commission
  • First delivered commissions, which become its record

This shapes the whole approach. With no inventory to sell, the first job is to make people care about a tradition and then invite them to create something with its makers.

Getting the categories right

Kinds of art, and kinds of maker

Two different questions have been mixed together. One is what kind of art KalaAnchal works with. The other is what kind of maker a person is within that art.

First question: which art world?

India's school curriculum calls these the "living art traditions": forms long labelled folk art, tribal art, ritual art or craft.5 They are a different world from gallery-led modern and contemporary art, with different training, routes to market and ways of setting prices.

Living traditional artsModern and contemporary fine art
What it includesFolk painting (Madhubani, Pattachitra), tribal or Adivasi art (Gond, Warli, Saura), court and temple traditions (miniature, Pichwai), and craft (embroidery, metal, wood)6, 7, 8Individual studio practice in painting, sculpture, installation and new media
How the maker is trainedIn the family or under a guru, within a communityUsually an art college, then studio practice
Whose visual languageA shared, inherited one, which each maker interpretsThe individual artist's own
Route to marketTraders, fairs, emporiums, government exhibitions, some galleriesGalleries, art fairs, auctions, advisors
How price is setMostly by size, detail and time. By name only for a few.By the artist's name and record
Access to art institutionsLimited. The President of India has noted that acclaimed Sohrai, Madhubani and Gond practitioners struggle to reach fine-arts institutions.9Built into the system

KalaAnchal's focus is the first column. Every maker in the network belongs to the living traditional arts. The line between the columns does blur at the top: a few Gond and Mithila artists now show in contemporary galleries and sell at auction, and critics remain divided on whether such work is traditional or contemporary.10, 11 That blur is an opportunity for co-creation, taken up later.

Within the first column, a specialist gallerist draws one more line: tribal art comes from India's recognised tribes, while other traditional art is heritage practice from non-tribal communities.12

Second question: which kind of maker, within the traditional arts?

The government's own ladder runs from the registered artisan to the State Award, the National Award and the Shilp Guru, its highest honour, which asks for at least 20 years of practice.13 In practice the difference between makers lies in what they do, more than in what they have been awarded.

Executes to a pattern

What they do
Carry out part or all of a piece to a design set by someone else.
Background
Household units and groups, many of them women working alongside home and farm duties.
Training
At home or through a training programme. Formal education usually low.
Recognition
Usually none in their own name.
How they earn
Piece rates. Often seasonal or part-time.
Route to market
Whoever brings the order.
What holds them back
Low and uncertain pay, debt, dependence on the contractor.
What KalaAnchal can add
Regular orders at fair rates as volume grows. Tri-Impact's support matters most here.

Recognition is not the same as market access

A recognised master often still sells through middlemen. In Jitwarpur, the best-known Madhubani village, with Padma Shri and National Award holders among its painters, artists report that middlemen bring the orders and take most of the profit, and a national awardee says the art has no proper channel of sale.14, 15, 16 So the three kinds of maker differ in role and skill. Dependence on middlemen runs across all three.

The three profiles are illustrative, for the team to test against the makers in the network. Sector context: India has an estimated 64.66 lakh handloom and handicraft artisans, 64% of them women.17 A 2026 Crafts Council of India study reports low education, high debt and weak market access among artisan households.18

  • Indigenous art is gaining recognition.The 2026 India Art Fair reflected growing interest in Indigenous art, with several stands devoted to it.23 A Gond work by a late master sold for a record Rs 18 crore hammer price in September 2026.24The category is being validated by fairs, galleries and auctions.
  • The market is broadening below the top.In 2025, 700 auction lots sold below Rs 1 lakh and 1,699 between Rs 1 lakh and Rs 10 lakh, which the report reads as new collectors entering.25There are buyers at the price levels where traditional art sits.
  • New collectors look beyond known names.Gallerists describe younger collectors as well informed and open to unfamiliar artists.26Room for artists without an auction record.
  • Buyers of Indian art are mostly in India.Gallerists told Art Basel that the large majority are Indian or based in India.27The home market is the main market.
  • Hotels are designing around place.A designer of a recent Taj property describes a shift to localisation and a layered use of regional art.28 Around 65% of IHCL's hotels in India are in Tier-2 and emerging destinations.29Demand for work rooted in the property's own region.
  • Public spaces are commissioning traditional art.Mumbai airport's T2 runs one of the largest public art programmes at an airport.30 A museum and Noida Metro placed folk and tribal murals across ten stations, painted by over 50 artists.31A visible, large-format category of commission.
  • Luxury brands are turning to Indian craft.Dior commissioned embroidered panels from over 300 Indian artisans through the Chanakya atelier, and Prada's Kolhapuri-style sandals set off a public debate about credit.32, 33Naming the maker is becoming a selling point.
  • Online alone does not close sales.Online sales were 15% of the global art market in 2025 after three years of decline.34Social media creates interest; conversations and visits close the commission.
  • The handmade sector is short of capital.78% of craft-led small businesses struggle for working capital and only one in ten has easy access to finance.35A constraint for the venture, and a gap funders know.
  • Government promotes tribal art.A network of 117 state-run outlets and regular fairs take this work to buyers.36More awareness, and a low-priced public alternative.

Four things they suggest

  • The venture speaks. In every case the business addresses the client. Okhai is the telling example: the seller is legally a non-profit and still meets customers as a brand.
  • Impact is stated as part of the business. It shows up as ownership, artist income or origin, in the venture's own voice.
  • Project work pays. The closest comparable earns two-fifths of its revenue from enterprise projects and calls that its growth driver.50
  • Most began narrow. One started with workshops, another with a single retailer as client, another by testing at exhibitions before launching.49, 54

What sets it apart

Why tradition first works, and what keeps it from being generic

Why it works

Devotional and Vastu themes

Devotional subjects are part of many of these traditions and are in demand for homes and hotels. They can be presented as what they are. A different way of doing it

What co-creation means for KalaAnchal

Bringing together the skills of different local art traditions, and where useful a designer or contemporary artist, to create contemporary work that no single tradition would produce alone.

There are precedents. An art residency in Odisha paired contemporary urban artists with rural artisans to make work for today's settings.84 A leading Gond artist has shown alongside a contemporary painter and says such collaborations push him to think in a contemporary way.85 One project has designers and artisans develop each work together from folklore.86 Bridge Bharat describes its own work as co-creating contemporary expressions of traditional painting.87

AdvantagesThings to handle with care
  • Work that is new, so it is hard to compare on price with ready-made folk art
  • Uses what the network has: range across traditions
  • Speaks to designers and hotels, who want something made for the space
  • Moves traditional makers toward the contemporary market, where prices follow the name
  • Authorship: whose work is it, and how is each maker credited?
  • Integrity: makers and communities need a say in how their tradition is used
  • Critics are divided on where such work belongs, traditional or contemporary10
  • It needs a creative lead who understands both the traditions and the client

How a co-creation could run

  1. Conversation. The client describes the space, the mood and the budget range.
  2. Concept. KalaAnchal suggests a tradition, an artist and an idea, with references.
  3. Sample. A small study or a section, for a fee.
  4. Agreement. Scope, price, timeline and an advance.
  5. Making. Progress shared with the client; the artist is named throughout.
  6. Delivery. Installed, with a record of origin and the story of the work.

Funding

The investor view

The team is exploring impact investment for KalaAnchal. That makes the venture's own record, and the name its first clients know, matter more.

What the sector evidence says

  • Grants and CSR on one side, investment on the other. Research on Indian impact enterprises reports that regulations keep CSR funds from flowing to them.91 Tri-Impact and KalaAnchal therefore draw on different money.
  • Capital for handmade businesses is scarce. Most craft-led small businesses in a 2023 survey needed working capital and growth capital they could not easily get.35
  • Impact investment in India is sizeable but uneven. Impact enterprises raised USD 2.9 billion in 2023, down from USD 6 billion the year before.92
  • Comparable ventures have raised money as businesses: one from an impact fund and a retailer, another from an accelerator and angel investors.65, 49

What an investor is likely to look for

  • A clear definition and a differentiator that is hard to copy
  • Clients, orders and repeat business in KalaAnchal's own name
  • Evidence of impact from someone other than the venture
  • A network of makers that competitors cannot easily reach
  • What one commission earns after the maker is paid
  • A team with someone accountable for delivery

Milestones that would make the case

  1. A definition and a redesigned website that state what KalaAnchal is.
  2. A growing audience for the tradition stories, and enquiries that come from it.
  3. A first paid commission, delivered on time, with the maker paid as agreed.
  4. A second commission from the same client, or from a referral.
  5. A first impact statement from Tri-Impact on what the artists gained.
